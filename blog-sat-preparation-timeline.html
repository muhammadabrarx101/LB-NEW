A realistic four-month SAT preparation plan

What each month should look like, and why cramming the last three weeks rarely moves a score.

SAT scores move with sustained practice, not with intensity. That is the single most useful thing to understand before planning your SAT test preparation, because it explains why a student who works two hours a week for four months usually outperforms one who works forty hours in the last fortnight.

Why four months

The digital SAT tests reasoning under time pressure across a broad content base. Two things have to happen: content gaps get filled, and the method becomes automatic. The first can be rushed. The second cannot — automaticity is a function of repetition spaced over weeks, and there is no shortcut around it.

Four months, at around three sessions a week, is enough for both. Shorter plans work if you are already close to your target. Longer plans tend to lose momentum.

Month one — find out where you actually are

Start with a full-length mock under real conditions, including timing and breaks. Not a section, not an untimed practice set — a complete paper. The score matters less than the error pattern: which question types you miss, whether you ran out of time, and whether wrong answers came from not knowing or from misreading.

Then spend the month on the content gaps that mock exposed. For most students this means specific Math topics and a handful of grammar rules, not the whole syllabus.

Month two — build the method

This is where technique gets installed. For Reading & Writing, that means a consistent approach to each question type rather than reading and hoping. For Math, it means recognising which of a small number of approaches a question is asking for, and knowing when plugging in numbers beats solving algebraically.

Work at this stage should be untimed or generously timed. Speed comes later; installing a method while rushing installs a rushed method.

Month three — add the clock

Sectional practice under real timing, with an error log kept by question type rather than by test. The log is the part students skip and the part that matters — patterns only become visible across several sessions, and you cannot fix a pattern you have not noticed.

Expect scores to dip slightly when timing is introduced. That is normal and not a sign the method is wrong.

Month four — full mocks

Complete papers under exam conditions, ideally at the time of day your real test is scheduled. Review every paper question by question, including the ones you got right by guessing — those are future mistakes waiting to happen.

By the final fortnight the work should be maintenance rather than new learning. Cramming new content in the last two weeks tends to unsettle a method that was working.

What to expect from your score

Improvement depends heavily on your baseline and on the work done between sessions. Students starting from a low baseline generally have more room, because the early gains come from content gaps that are quick to fill. Students already scoring well are fighting for marginal questions and improve more slowly.

Be sceptical of any programme promising a specific point gain before it has seen you sit a paper. We will give you a range after your diagnostic mock, and we would rather be accurate than encouraging.

The digital format changes some things

The current SAT is digital and section-adaptive: your performance on the first module of a section affects the difficulty of the second. Practically, this means the early questions carry more weight than students assume, and rushing the opening of a section to buy time at the end is a worse trade than it used to be.

It is also shorter than the paper test, with a built-in calculator and reference sheet. Practice on the official digital platform rather than paper materials, so the interface is not a variable on test day.

How our programme is built

Around forty-eight sessions across four months, three a week, following exactly the sequence above: diagnostic mock, content gaps, method, timed practice, full mocks. Both Reading & Writing and Math are covered, with individual weak-area work rather than uniform coverage, and performance tracked across mocks so progress is visible rather than assumed.
