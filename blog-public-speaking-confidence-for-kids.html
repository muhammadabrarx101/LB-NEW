Public speaking for kids: how to build confidence

Confident speakers are made, not born. Small, safe steps — and plenty of chances to speak — turn nervous children into children who enjoy being heard.

Quick answer

Build confidence in small steps: speaking to family, then a small group, then larger audiences. Practise with stories and topics children enjoy, teach simple structure, and treat nerves as normal, not as failure.

Start small

StepExample
1Telling a family member about their day in detail
2Retelling a favourite story at dinner
3Speaking in a small group of 3–5
4A short presentation to a class

Skipping straight to step four is where many children decide they "hate public speaking".

Use stories

Storytelling is public speaking in its most natural form. Children who learn to tell a story with a beginning, middle and end — and to pause, vary their voice and look at their audience — carry those skills into every presentation. Our Tales and Telling course is built around this.

Teach a simple structure

  1. Hook — a question, a surprising fact, a short story
  2. Three main points
  3. A clear ending — what you want people to remember

Structure reduces nerves because children know what comes next.

Practise at home

  • Short "show and tell" talks about something they love
  • Recording a talk and watching it back (gently)
  • Family debates on light topics — cats or dogs?

Handling nerves

Explain that nerves are normal — even experienced speakers feel them. Slow breathing, a glass of water and practising the first sentence until it is automatic all help.

Why small groups matter

In a large class, shy children can avoid speaking for months. In a group of five, everyone speaks every session, and confidence grows quickly because the audience feels safe.

Praise the right things

Praise effort and specific improvements: "You looked up at us much more this time." Avoid comparing them with more confident siblings or classmates.

Frequently asked questions

How can I help my shy child speak in public?
Start small — family, then a small group — use stories they enjoy, teach a simple structure and praise specific progress.

At what age should children learn public speaking?
Children can start with storytelling and show-and-tell from a young age, building to presentations as they grow.

How do I help my child with stage fright?
Treat nerves as normal, practise the opening until it is automatic, and build up gradually through smaller audiences.

Do small groups help children speak more?
Yes. In small groups every child speaks regularly, which builds confidence much faster than large classes.
